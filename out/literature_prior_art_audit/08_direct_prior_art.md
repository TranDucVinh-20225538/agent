# Direct prior-art deep dive: the two papers that matter

Neither reaches Level 3. Both were independently re-verified by the orchestrating session directly (not just relayed from a search subagent) because the novelty verdict hinges on them.

## 1. Turk, "Counterfactual Evaluation Reveals Hidden Capability Profiles in Clinical LLMs and Agents" — arXiv:2605.30590

**Verified directly:** title, single author (Matt Turk), submission date May 28, 2026, venue RLEval @ ACM CAIS 2026 (workshop, invited talk) — confirmed via `WebFetch` on both the abstract page and the full HTML body (`arxiv.org/html/2605.30590v1`).

**Design, as the paper itself describes it:** twelve pre-registered interventions across five families (biomarker flip, prior-treatment-failure addition, biomarker removal, surgery-status toggle, stage change) are applied to oncology tumor-board case packets. For each (model, intervention, case) tuple, a baseline recommendation (E0) and an intervened recommendation (E1) are generated by the same model under the same instruction. A judge scores {0, 0.5, 1.0} — no change / acknowledged-but-unchanged / updated correctly in the pre-registered direction — this is the Causal Sensitivity Score (CSS). Separately, the same case set has a pre-existing coverage-based rubric, the Consensus Match Score (CMS).

**The single decisive technical question, resolved by direct fetch of the paper's full text:** is CMS recomputed on the intervened (E1) cases and differenced against baseline CMS per instance, the way Paper 1 computes S_CF − S_Base? **No.** Quoted directly from the paper: *"CMS measures output coverage (does the recommendation overlap the consensus?); CSS, by contrast, measures input responsiveness."* CMS is computed only on baseline performance. The paper compares CSS and CMS **across models, in aggregate**, via Spearman rank correlation (ρ = −0.49, exact permutation p = 0.36 at n = 6, explicitly flagged by the authors as underpowered) and via each metric's own score spread (CSS spans 16.4pp, CMS spans 13.1pp) — not via a per-case ΔCMS computed on the same paired E0/E1 instance.

The paper's own motivating example, quoted directly: *"An oncology AI that proposes FOLFIRINOX for a pancreatic case scores equally well on coverage-based metrics whether the patient is treatment-naive or whether it just always proposes FOLFIRINOX for pancreatic cases."* This is qualitatively the same intuition as Paper 1's Type B (a score stays flat/high despite — or regardless of — whether the underlying determining state was actually tracked), but it is illustrated anecdotally and via the aggregate rank-correlation finding, not via a per-instance score_delta computed on the same paired run the way Paper 1 reports it.

Also found: a case where a ReAct-style tool-using agent "retrieves the same chart sections and still fails to update its recommendation" — genuine information access without state-responsive output, structurally identical in shape to Paper 1's Type B mechanism (Claude `preference_inference-f018`: correctly reads one channel, doesn't update the other).

**Why this is Level 2, not Level 3:**
1. Different domain — clinical case-file text reasoning, not computer-use/GUI agents; no environment/application state, no screen/UI observation, no accessibility tree or file-system ground truth.
2. CSS is itself the tracking measurement, not a construct kept separate from a third-party score. Paper 1 treats an existing benchmark's own score as a black box under test and computes its own delta; Turk's paper compares two *different* metrics (CSS vs CMS) via aggregate cross-model correlation, not one metric's own paired within-instance delta.
3. Two-way construct (tracking vs. coverage), not Paper 1's three-way (completion / tracking / score-sensitivity).
4. No Type A / score-sensitive / Type B taxonomy; the paper's central result is a rank-correlation divergence across models, not a per-instance classification scheme.

**What this means for Paper 1:** this is the single most important paper to cite and differentiate explicitly. It independently establishes the same core intuition and a structurally similar (though not identical) paired-intervention design in an adjacent domain, published earlier (May 2026, vs. this program's Stage-4/Phase-B work). It should not be treated as a footnote — it deserves its own differentiating paragraph (see `11_recommended_related_work.md`).

## 2. Dong, Ma, Wang, Li, Liu, Deng, Zhan, Qian — "How Benchmarks Mis-Score Computer-Use Agents" — arXiv:2607.28367

**Verified directly:** full title, full 8-author list, submission date July 30, 2026, and the complete abstract — confirmed via `WebFetch` on the abstract page.

**Full abstract, quoted verbatim:** *"Computer-use agents (CUA) are being deployed to browse the web and operate desktop software, yet their benchmark scores are still commonly produced by brittle scripted oracles. A score is the output of a pipeline in which tasks can be stale, trajectories can omit decisive visual evidence, evaluators can reject valid alternatives, and aggregate reports can hide the cause of failure. We organize these problems into a reliability framework spanning task construction, trajectory observation, scoring, and reporting. We then audit 150 public failure-scored trajectories from five web, enterprise-workflow, and desktop-control benchmarks, find that 15.3% of FAIL verdicts are wrong: 10.7% are evaluator false negatives and 4.7% are broken tasks. For genuine failures, a three-tier diagnostic taxonomy shows that verification/feedback and planning failures dominate execution/grounding errors, while a single scalar success rate can not explain. We connect these findings to newer long-horizon CUA benchmarks and derive stage-specific design rules for CUA evaluation."*

**Confirmed: this is a retrospective audit of existing recorded trajectories, not a controlled paired intervention.** The 150 trajectories audited are pre-existing FAIL-scored runs from five already-published benchmarks (OSWorld, WebArena, WorkArena, VisualWebArena, AssistantBench per the subagent's cross-referenced reading of the method section). Two LLM judges (plus blind human review on a subsample) relabel each FAIL as genuine-failure / evaluator-false-negative / broken-task. There is no environment-state manipulation, no E0/E1 pairing of the same task, and no separate measurement of "does the agent's answer track a manipulated determining set" independent of the benchmark's own score — the paper audits whether the *original* scorer's verdict on the *original* (unperturbed) trajectory was correct, via relabeling, not via re-running under an intervention.

**Why this is Level 2, not higher:** same domain (CUA) and the same general concern (benchmark scores can be wrong/unfaithful), but a fundamentally different method — post-hoc relabeling of existing failure cases vs. Paper 1's forward, controlled, paired-intervention design. It also does not distinguish "state tracking" from "benchmark score" as two separately measured constructs — it relabels the score itself as right/wrong against human judgment, collapsing the two into one corrected verdict.

**Why it still must be cited prominently:** its title alone ("How Benchmarks Mis-Score Computer-Use Agents") is close enough to Paper 1's framing that a reviewer will assume overlap on sight; Related Work must address it directly and explain the methodological distinction rather than let a reviewer discover the near-title-match unaddressed.
