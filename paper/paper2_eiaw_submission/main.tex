\begin{abstract}
Computer-use agents are compared by a completion-conditioned score: an episode must terminate, and a rubric judge then assigns a number.
We ask whether that number and a state-tracking measure select the same agent, on a universe and analysis plan frozen before any outcome existed.
Three agents each ran $57$ pre-registered legs under one instrument.
Coverage was the first result rather than a nuisance: valid counterfactual pairs, which require both legs to reach a canonical \texttt{DONE}, numbered $9$ for GPT-5.5, $8$ for Qwen3.8-Flash, and $1$ for Claude Opus~4.6, so only two agents met the pre-registered threshold $n_{\min}=3$ and the confirmatory selection test was, by its own pre-registered rule, not evaluated.
Binary state tracking was $0$ on \emph{every} valid pair of every agent, leaving score-versus-tracking calibration degenerate rather than weak.
In the remaining exploratory comparison the two measurement layers did not agree: on a four-task common support the score favoured Flash by $+46.5$ points (95\% CI $[21.0,\,72.0]$), while graded tracking favoured GPT-5.5 by $-0.042$ (95\% CI $[-0.125,\,0.000]$) --- an aggregate reversal that is strict on one of four tasks, tied on the other three, and erased by removing that one task.
We therefore report measurement instability under this instrument, not an agent ranking: the observed ordering depends on which layer and which support is used.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Leaderboards for computer-use agents (CUAs) report a completion score: the episode ended and a rubric assigned a number~\citep{jang2026mypcbench,xie2024osworld}.
Selecting an agent from such a leaderboard presumes that the score orders agents the way the property one cares about would order them.
Prior work in this line showed that, for individual episodes, task completion, counterfactual state tracking, and score sensitivity can come apart~\citep{shao2026protocolvalidity,turk2026counterfactualclinical}.
That leaves the decision-relevant question open: when the measurement layer changes, does the \emph{choice} of agent change?

This paper answers that question on a sealed universe with an analysis plan fixed in advance, and reports what actually happened, which is not a clean disagreement and not a clean agreement.
Two findings dominate and both are about the instrument rather than the agents.

First, \textbf{coverage decided who could be compared at all}.
The unit of a counterfactual comparison is a \emph{pair} of episodes, base and intervened, and a pair enters the analysis only if both legs terminate canonically.
Termination rates differed so sharply across agents that one frontier agent contributed a single usable pair out of a possible $19$, which under the pre-registered threshold removed it from ranking and reduced the roster below the minimum required for the confirmatory test.

Second, \textbf{the two layers produced different aggregate preferences but at the edge of one layer's resolution}.
Among the two agents with enough support, the conventional score strongly preferred one agent while graded tracking preferred the other; that reversal, however, is carried entirely by one task out of four and the tracking measure is tied on the rest.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~A pre-registered, coverage-first audit in which the valid-pair count is reported as a finding rather than a denominator.
(2)~An instrument whose termination channel is \emph{fail-closed} and \emph{independent of the judge}, so that ``scored well without closing the episode'' is an observation rather than a bookkeeping artefact.
(3)~An exploratory two-layer comparison reported with its own fragility: the strict/tied decomposition, the single task that carries the reversal, the resolution limit of the tracking measure, and the flip between per-agent and common support.
The claim is methodological and scoped to this instrument; we do not offer a ranking and we do not estimate a population rate.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Frozen universe and plan.}
Tasks, agents, interventions, seed, step budget, and the analysis rules were written and committed before execution.
The inclusion threshold ($n_{\min}=3$ valid pairs to be ranked), the rule that fewer than three ranked agents means the confirmatory selection test is \emph{not evaluated}, the prohibition on optional stopping, and the form in which a two-agent comparison may appear were all fixed in advance of any outcome.
We note this because every headline number below is a null or a partial result, and the value of such results depends entirely on the plan predating them.

\paragraph{Pairs and interventions.}
For task $t$, $E_t^{0}$ is the seeded guest state and $E_t^{1}=I_t(E_t^{0})$ applies a locked patch to a pre-registered determining set $D_t$.
Instruction, interface, screenshot size, and judge are held fixed; guest probes confirm that gold moved.
Each task contributes legs $G0$ (base) and $G1$ (intervened); a third variant is executed but excluded from pairing by pre-registration.
A pair is \emph{valid} iff both $G0$ and $G1$ terminate canonically, and $\A_i$ denotes agent $i$'s valid pairs.
Execution failures are never recoded as tracking failures.

\paragraph{Two measurement layers.}
The conventional layer is the base-leg rubric $S^{0}\in\{0,\dots,100\}$, i.e.\ the number a leaderboard would report on the unmodified world; base and intervened scores are deliberately not averaged.
The tracking layer is a graded state-tracking score $\STS$, computed by extracting reported values $\hat D$ from the final answer and matching them against guest gold under per-component typed rules, with no judge in the loop.
Binary track $Y=1$ requires every positive-weight component to match on \emph{both} legs.
The extraction code was frozen at a recorded commit \emph{before} any aggregate $\STS$ was computed, and was not revised afterwards.

\paragraph{Termination is a separate channel, and it fails closed.}
Completion is read only from the last recorded trajectory action.
The classifier does not consult the judge's output when deciding termination, so episode closure and task score are derived from disjoint inputs; and it is fail-closed, meaning a missing, malformed, or unreadable trajectory can only be \emph{excluded} from $\A$, never converted into an apparent completion.
Both properties matter for how the numbers may be read: the reported $|\A_i|$ are \textbf{lower bounds}, and a high rubric score on a non-terminating episode cannot be an artefact of one channel leaking into the other.
An earlier defect in this project, in which a truthy \texttt{done} field was also set by crash and failure rows, was remediated before the first leg reported here, so no number below was reclassified after the fact.

\paragraph{Substrate disclosure.}
All three lanes ran the same generic XML tool protocol over one chat-completions endpoint.
This is \emph{not} the vendors' native computer-use instruments: the GPT-5.5 and Claude lanes were executed on the shared protocol after native tool-ownership validation failed or was unavailable on the run host.
Comparability is therefore within this roster and instrument only, and is not comparability against natively instrumented results for the same model names.

Claude terminated on four legs, and those four fall on three distinct tasks, so exactly one task has both legs terminating: $|\A|=1$ out of $19$ possible pairs.
Under the pre-registered threshold $n_{\min}=3$ it is reported but not ranked.
With one further agent excluded before execution for instrument and transport mismatch, the ranked roster is two agents, which is fewer than three, and therefore \textbf{the confirmatory selection test is not evaluated} --- a stated outcome of the plan rather than a failed experiment.

Two readings must be resisted here.
Claude's mean $S^{0}$ on $\A$ is the highest in the table, but it is a single pair on the one task the agent solved and closed; it is a coverage artefact, exactly the selection pressure the plan anticipated, and not evidence that the agent scores best.
Conversely the non-terminating legs are not tracking failures: they are absent observations, and recoding them as $Y=0$ is forbidden by the plan.

\section{Layer A: calibration is degenerate, not weak}
\label{sec:layera}

The intended calibration analysis asks how well $S^{0}$ predicts $P(Y=1)$ on valid pairs.
It cannot be estimated, for a stronger reason than small $n$: \textbf{$Y=0$ on all $18$ valid pairs across all three agents}.
No pair matched every positive-weight component of $D$ on both legs.
There is no positive class, so $S^{0}$ cannot separate $Y=1$ from $Y=0$, and any statement of the form ``score is (mis)calibrated to tracking'' is unavailable on this sample.

The graded measure is correspondingly near its floor: mean $\STS$ on $\A$ is $0.130$, $0.229$, and $0$ (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}).
This is the context in which the next section's differences must be read.
A gap between $\STS=0.250$ and $\STS=0.208$ is a gap between two small residuals, not between a reliable agent and an unreliable one.

\section{Exploratory two-layer comparison}
\label{sec:selection}

Because only two agents were rankable, the comparison below is reported in the single form the plan permits: signed differences on the common support, with a paired bootstrap, labelled \textbf{exploratory}, and not presented as the confirmatory test.
The common support is $\Acap=\{$\task{counterfactual-f010}, \task{preference\_inference-f014}, \task{retrieval-f002}, \task{retrieval-f009}$\}$, $|\Acap|=4$.

Aggregate means on $\Acap$ point in opposite directions: $S^{0}$ is $49.5$ for GPT-5.5 versus $96.0$ for Flash, so $\arg\max S^{0}$ is Flash; $\STS$ is $0.250$ versus $0.208$, so $\arg\max\STS$ is GPT-5.5.
A paired bootstrap over tasks (seed $20260904$, $B=5000$) gives $\Delta S^{0}=+46.5$ with 95\% CI $[21.0,\,72.0]$ and $\Delta\STS=-0.042$ with 95\% CI $[-0.125,\,0.000]$, differences taken as Flash minus GPT-5.5.

On three of four tasks $\Delta\STS$ is exactly zero: the tracking layer is \emph{indifferent} between the two agents, not opposed to the score.
Only \task{retrieval-f009} is a strict reversal.
The aggregate $\Delta\STS$ is therefore driven entirely by that one task, and removing it makes the $\STS$ comparison a tie.
A leave-one-pair-out check reports exactly this and nothing more: it is a sensitivity analysis confirming the same single-task concentration, not a second, independent piece of evidence.

\paragraph{The tracking layer is coarse at this scale.}
Across these pairs $\STS$ takes only the values $\{0.000,\,0.333,\,0.500\}$, because each task contributes a handful of typed components.
The honest reading is that the reversal sits at the resolution limit of the measure: with this few components per task, $\STS$ has little room to express a small disagreement, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the layer is disagreeing or merely failing to discriminate.
We raise the objection rather than wait for it, and we do not claim to settle it on four tasks.

\paragraph{The common support is not a neutral slice.}
Restricting to $\Acap$ moves GPT-5.5's mean $S^{0}$ from $69.3$ on its own nine pairs down to $49.5$, while Flash's is essentially unchanged ($95.8\to96.0$).
The shared tasks are thus adversarial to GPT-5.5 on the score layer.
The direction flips for tracking: on each agent's own $\A$, Flash is higher on \emph{both} layers ($\STS$ $0.229$ versus $0.130$), whereas on $\Acap$ GPT-5.5 is higher on $\STS$.
Both denominators are reported and neither was chosen after inspecting which looked better; the discrepancy between them is itself the result, namely that the selection question is sensitive to which tasks each agent happened to finish.

\section{Completion-conditioned observability}
\label{sec:completion}

Conditioning on termination changes which trajectories the downstream measurement can see at all, and the direction of that effect is worth stating precisely because the intuitive guess is wrong.
On average, excluded cells score \emph{lower} than included ones: mean $S$ off $\A$ is $46.4$, $44.9$, and $26.1$ for GPT-5.5, Flash, and Claude, against $69.3$, $95.8$, and $100$ on $\A$.
The completion filter is therefore conservative for the calibration question rather than flattering.

What survives that averaging is a small set of sharp cases: $2$, $2$, and $1$ excluded cells scored $\ge 90$ with no canonical termination.
Because the terminal channel is independent of the judge (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), these are genuine observations of substantial rubric-visible progress without episode closure, not scoring accidents.
They are descriptive: none enters $\arg\max$, and none is converted into a pair.
Their significance is that a completion-conditioned protocol cannot express them, so an agent whose failure mode is non-closure is measured mostly by its absence.

\paragraph{Instrument asymmetry.}
One further caveat bounds the coverage numbers.
A parser canonicalisation that rescues near-miss tool syntax was enumerated from one agent's dialect, so it is more forgiving of that agent.
Re-deriving terminal outcomes with those rescues treated as rejections moves Flash from $|\A|=8$ to $7$; the other lanes are unaffected because the canonicalisation does not fire on their output.
The pre-registered dual analysis is reported for this reason rather than assumed away.

\section{Discussion}

The result of this audit is a measurement problem, not a ranking.
Under this frozen instrument, on the four-task common support, with two rankable agents, and with binary tracking identically zero throughout, CUA reliability is not adequately characterised by a single completion-conditioned score: the ordering that measurement produces depends on which layer is used and on which tasks each agent finished.
We state the scope inside the claim deliberately.
We have shown instability of this instrument on this universe; we have not shown that CUA benchmarks in general are invalid, and the data here could not support that.

Both quantities compared here are proxies.
$S^{0}$ is a rubric judge's number and $\STS$ is a typed match against guest gold through an extractor; neither is independently established ground truth, so a disagreement between them licenses no inference about which is correct.
This is why we decline both directions of the tempting conclusion: the evidence does not say one agent tracks state better, only that the proxies do not order the pair invariantly under the observed support.

The constructive implication is for the design of the next measurement rather than for any leaderboard.
Three properties of this instrument turned out to carry the analysis: termination read from a fail-closed channel, scoring read from a channel that never consults it, and an extraction step frozen before aggregates were computed.
Those made the null results interpretable.
What they could not supply is a validated target: with $Y=0$ everywhere, the tracking layer produced an ordering but no positive instance against which to check that the ordering means what its name says.
A reliability measure for agents that frequently do not close episodes therefore needs two things this study did not have: a target defined without reference to episode completion, and positive and negative controls that can separate genuine reliability signal from artefacts of support and protocol observability.

\paragraph{Broader impacts.}
Making coverage and layer dependence visible discourages reading a single completion score as a certificate of state tracking.
The same paired design could be turned on proprietary graders; we intend it as a diagnostic of measurement, not as an exploit.

\section{Related work}

OSWorld~\citep{xie2024osworld}, WebArena~\citep{zhou2024webarena}, and VisualWebArena~\citep{koh2024visualwebarena} score agents with completion rubrics; MyPCBench~\citep{jang2026mypcbench} provides the seeded personal desktop that our interventions reuse.
OSWorld and WebArena are Apache-2.0, VisualWebArena is MIT, and MyPCBench is MIT with an OSWorld-derived harness under Apache-2.0; we omit URLs to preserve anonymity.
None pairs $E^{0}$ with $E^{1}=I(E^{0})$ to separate state tracking from the score, and none reports valid-pair coverage as a quantity that can decide whether a comparison exists.
\citet{shao2026protocolvalidity} audit whether the intended capability remains necessary for a score; we ask instead whether two measurement layers select the same agent.
\citet{turk2026counterfactualclinical} show that coverage-style scores can hide update failures under controlled mutations; the completion-conditioning we report is the same hazard displaced into the eligibility rule rather than the rubric.
\citet{bellibatlu2026judgesense} measure judge instability under paraphrase; our instability is with respect to the measurement layer and the analysis support, with the judge held fixed.
The paired design follows interventionist language~\citep{pearl2009causality}; we identify no model internals.

\section{Limitations}

Three agents, one environment, one instrument, $57$ legs each, and $18$ valid pairs in total.
Only two agents were rankable and the confirmatory selection test was not evaluated, so the two-layer comparison is exploratory throughout.
Binary tracking is zero everywhere, which makes the calibration analysis degenerate and leaves $\STS$ unvalidated against any positive instance.
The common support has four tasks; the aggregate $\STS$ reversal rests on one of them and disappears without it; and $\STS$ takes only three distinct values on these pairs.
Reported $|\A|$ are lower bounds by construction, and the near-miss asymmetry moves one lane's count by one pair.
The lanes ran a shared generic tool protocol rather than vendor-native computer-use instruments, so these are results for the named models \emph{on this substrate}.
Per-step guest state was not retained, so no analysis here can condition on in-episode environment state.

\section{Conclusion}

On a universe and analysis plan frozen in advance, the agent that a completion-conditioned score selects is not the agent a graded state-tracking measure selects, but the disagreement is exploratory, concentrated in a single task, and measured at the resolution limit of the tracking layer --- and it sits on top of a coverage asymmetry severe enough that one frontier agent yielded a single usable pair and the confirmatory test could not be run at all.
The finding we are willing to defend is the instability itself: under this instrument, which agent looks better depends on the measurement layer and the analysis support.
Validating a reliability target that does not require episode completion, against explicit controls, is the prerequisite for turning that instability into a measurement.
